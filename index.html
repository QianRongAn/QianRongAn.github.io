Projects

Meta-ACDC · TCR off-target risk prediction (graph deep learning / computational immunology)

Independent research project (AI-driven) | Python / PyTorch | 2026 | Open source on GitHub

Deep learning predicts binding specificity between a T-cell receptor (TCR) and a peptide, so off-target peptides that could injure healthy tissue, or be lethal, can be recognized before treatment. The motivation is the 2013 MAGE-A3 TCR therapy fatality: the lethal off-target peptide and the target peptide differed by a backbone RMSD of only 0.285 Å, and the difference was side-chain chemistry. The project closes the loop from multi-source data pipelines, model design, ablation, domain adaptation, and active learning through to a clinical gold-standard retrospective test.

Model and methods

  • E(3)-equivariant graph neural network (EGNN), implemented from scratch. A TCR-pMHC complex is built as an interface graph (residues as nodes, edges within 8 Å interface distance, interface cutoff 10 Å). Node features combine physicochemical properties and residue identity. Edge features use a distance radial basis (RBF) plus a side-chain van der Waals contact histogram (C-C / C-heteroatom / heteroatom-heteroatom / total, 4 dimensions). Rotation-invariance error is below 0.002. There is no PyTorch Geometric dependency, and the model trains on a GTX 1050 Ti with 4 GB.
  • DECOY negative-sample protocol. Because true negatives are scarce, two decoys are constructed: a displacement decoy (the peptide is translated 6 Å out of the MHC groove, breaking shape) and a graft decoy (backbone coordinates are kept and the side-chain chemical identity is replaced, recreating the titin lethal cross-reactivity mechanism). This gives a controlled discrimination task and a leak-resistant control protocol.
  • Readout design. Besides global mean and global max pooling, peptide nodes are mean-pooled on their own (the peptide is the variable under test), and a coordinate-norm term is added. Peptide pairs with the same sequence identity (GILGLVFTL 0.938 vs GILEFVFTL 0.475) show that the model learned chemical specificity rather than a similarity proxy.

Experiments and core findings

  • Ablation locates the representation bottleneck. With backbone coordinates (Cα) only, graft-decoy AUROC is 0.500 (chance), and more data does not help. A three-part diagnosis rules out the usual fixes one by one (peptide-aware pooling 0.498, residue-identity embedding 0.499, sequence-plus-graph hybrid 0.905, which is worse than structure alone at 0.933). The problem is the representation, not the architecture.
  • Side-chain contact features break through. After adding the side-chain van der Waals contact histogram, graft decoys move from 0.500 to 0.933 and displacement decoys from 0.946 to 0.980. Systematic ablation ranks feature necessity (removing the contact histogram falls back to 0.501, decisive; removing physicochemical scalars 0.606; removing residue identity 0.899). A lethal interface that has the same shape and different chemistry becomes separable.
  • The Rashomon extrapolation crisis, found and resolved. Models with the same decoy-task performance completely change the ranking of AlphaFold3-predicted structures after one retrain (pairwise Spearman 0.17 / −0.24 / NaN). A cross-instance stability gate is proposed (report a ranking only when multi-seed mean Spearman is at least 0.5) and generalized as a method for the “predicted structure + learned score” paradigm.
  • Domain adaptation repairs unstable extrapolation. AF3-predicted native complexes, mixed with same-protocol decoys, are added to training (node set pinned, donors deduplicated against leakage). Native positives grow from 25 to 144, and cross-instance rank consistency rises monotonically from 0.447 to 0.802 (95% CI 0.719–0.857). Domain adaptation also improves the crystal task (graft 0.918 to 0.98). Degenerate seeds that collapse to a constant output are detected and removed, confirming that stability equals data scale times seed quality.
  • Active-learning sampling loop. An EIG/BALD acquisition function simulates the loop “AI picks a library, experiment validates, model updates” on a pool of 4.65 million candidate peptides. A 6% sampling budget yields a 2.0× gain in positive recall (better than random, entropy, and variance baselines). A batch-size ablation finds large batches (3600×2, 24.5%) better than small batches over many rounds (300×20, 21.6%), opposite the usual advice in the literature. The gain is threshold-dependent (it disappears under a strict 0.9 prescreen and remains 1.76× under a loose 0.5 prescreen).
  • Clinical gold-standard retrospective test. On the fatal MAGE-A3 TCR therapy case (PDB 5BRZ), the target peptide ranks 1st. The two lethal off-target peptides (MAGE-A12 and the titin mimic) score only 0.02 / 0.06 lower, so known dangerous peptides are conservatively not missed. A third high-risk off-target is partly missed (0.679) and is reported as such. Score temperature calibration is good (ECE 0.02–0.06).
  • Interpretability. Gradient attribution plus per-position alanine/tryptophan scanning shows that replacing the whole peptide drops the score from 0.989 to 0.001 (the model does look at chemistry), but the largest single-point mutation Δ is only 0.02. Compatibility is a distributed representation with no single lethal hotspot, so removing cross-reactivity needs coordinated changes at multiple sites.

Data pipeline and engineering

  • Multi-source integration. VDJdb (199,000 TCR-peptide pairs), IEDB (2.3 million MHC presentation records), STCRDab (291 crystal complexes to 423 training complexes), and the UniProt human proteome (20,431 proteins to 4.65 million unique peptides). A CIF/PDB structure parser is implemented in-house.
  • Cross-reactivity map. 291 crystal complexes are mapped to VDJdb by chain fingerprint: 281/291 map, 2,309 CDR3 hits, 1,517 hetero-epitopes (cross-reactivity evidence). 179/281 TCRs have a cross-reactivity record (median 2 hetero-epitopes, maximum 8), a quantitative demonstration that cross-reactivity is common rather than exceptional.
  • MHC prescreen benchmark. Local MHCflurry scores 50 known binders plus 50 composition-shuffled decoys (a hard protocol). A top-2% threshold keeps 56% of true binders and compresses the candidate pool about 50×, reducing the load on structure prediction.
  • Methodological rigor. A first-train AUROC of 0.988 was self-caught as decoy-identity leakage (graft decoys had no atoms, so the contact feature exposed identity), judged invalid, and redone (0.933 after the fix). The first 17 AF3 jobs were voided because of chain-concatenation errors, and a rule was added that automated output must be spot-checked as structure. Negative results and pitfalls are written into an honesty log.
  • Engineering delivery. 11 CLI entry points, 16 regression tests plus 19 delivery-package tests plus 10 import regressions, a reproducible Docker environment (accelerated domestic mirrors), a zero-dependency web safety board, a one-command health check, and a from-scratch reproduction SOP.

MedLit Evidence · evidence-based medical literature QA and retraction-risk prediction (NLP / retrieval / machine learning, open source)

Independently designed and built | FastAPI + native front end | Open source on GitHub (MIT)

An evidence-based question-answering system for medical editors and clinical researchers. The core idea is to model disagreement in the evidence explicitly: it does not emit a false consensus, every sentence is anchored to a real PMID, and a retraction-risk prediction pipeline is built in (23,279 labeled papers). It runs with zero API keys. The backend has only three dependencies.

Retrieval and NLP

  • Retrieval path. BM25 is implemented in pure Python (no sklearn/nltk). Tokenization is bilingual (English word-level plus stop words; Chinese characters plus bigrams). More than 70 Chinese medical terms are mapped to English query terms (after mapping, online recall for a Chinese question rises from 0 to more than 8,600 hits). The four PICO elements (population, intervention, comparator, outcome) are structured and then searched against PubMed (38 million papers).
  • Three-way conclusion polarity. Conclusion sentences are taken first from the CONCLUSIONS/FINDINGS/INTERPRETATION spans of the abstract (DISCUSSION/LIMITATION excluded). Polarity uses a three-layer rule of strong phrases first plus negation awareness, so phrases such as “no significant benefit” are not misread as neutral. Polarity counts detect conflicting evidence (support and opposition both present, then an explicit “the evidence disagrees” label lists the paper counts on each side), against the false consensus of a general large language model.
  • Interpretable confidence score. A 0–100 score with an explicit formula (evidence-level base score as a weighted average over all papers, plus or minus conclusion consistency, volume, and recency). The score can be taken apart and questioned. One meta-analysis cannot hide a large amount of low-quality evidence.
  • Integrity check. PublicationType and CommentsCorrections metadata identify retracted, expression-of-concern, and corrected papers. A red badge warns, those papers are kept out of the conclusion tally, and the confidence score is penalized.

Machine learning and experimental rigor (retraction-risk pipeline)

  • Labeled dataset. Retracted, concern, and correction records are collected against normal controls, stratified by year band (12,978 positive / 15,000 negative, aligned year by year), growing from a 360-paper pilot (same journal, same year) to 23,279 clean papers. The collection script supports resume-from-checkpoint.
  • Control-group redesign. The first 76 “normal” controls mixed in 33 editorials and 6 news items. Editorials are almost never retracted for data fabrication, so the model would only learn “an editorial is not a research paper.” Controls were rebuilt as normal research papers sampled by year, excluding non-research types.
  • Two layers of label leakage, found and fixed. The first layer is explicit (publication type literally says Retracted Publication; those rows are removed). The second is hidden (a retraction record’s abstract is often emptied or replaced by a retraction notice, so “missing abstract” itself leaks the label and contaminates length features). 4,345 papers with too-short abstracts and 277 replaced records are filtered, and digit density is normalized per thousand words. After the fix, the small-sample claim that bibliographic records were “thinner” is falsified and corrected in the report.
  • Model and evaluation. LightGBM is the main model: AUC 0.856 ± 0.004 / PR-AUC 0.812 ± 0.008 / F1 0.707, ahead of logistic regression (0.795) and random forest (0.828). Besides 5-fold stratified cross-validation, a temporal extrapolation test is added (train on ≤2024, predict 2025+, AUC 0.715), which is closer to real use than cross-validation.
  • SHAP attribution and artifact diagnosis. The SHAP plot shows publication year as the top feature, but the red/blue split exposes a censoring artifact on sight (older papers have simply had longer to be retracted). The real signal: retracted papers use more statistical terms (median 1.0 vs 0), have longer titles (120 vs 110 characters), and meta-analyses and basic-science papers retract more often, while the intuitive “hyped wording” contributes almost nothing. Funding is a protective signal.
  • Negative-result experiment (citation-signal control). 78,481 OpenAlex citation signals are collected concurrently (36 papers per second, 44% hit rate). Simple citation signals (citation count, author count) lose to the year artifact at predicting future retraction (temporal PR-AUC 0.194 vs 0.578), because citation counts are censored the same way and controversial papers are cited more. That negative result redirects the main line of work toward relational reasoning.
  • Model in the product. The risk model is wired into retrieval. Every paper returned for a question carries a live “risk N%” badge in four colors. Feature construction and training share the same vocabulary code. If the model file is missing, the badge hides and the main flow still works.

Engineering and performance

  • Performance. A cold-start question takes 7.8 seconds (including two PubMed round trips). An in-process TTL cache hit takes 6 milliseconds. Requests are scheduled under PubMed rate limits. If the network drops, the system falls back to a built-in corpus of 11 landmark studies, so a demo never goes blank.
  • Quality and design. 30 unit tests cover retrieval, study-design recognition, evidence grading, PICO, polarity, conflict detection, and the integrity check. An LLM is a pluggable upgrade (an OpenAI-compatible interface; when configured, answers become generative RAG, and without it the system falls back to extractive answers). Extractive answers are hallucination-free by construction.

Master's Thesis: Bioinformatics Analysis of COVID-19-Associated Immune Repertoire and Specific TCRs

Chapter 1: Introduction

1.1 Immunomics Background

Immune System: The immune system is divided into two components: innate immunity and adaptive immunity. Adaptive immunity primarily involves T cells (TCR), B cells, and MHC molecules.

Innate and adaptive branches of the immune system

1.2 Immune Repertoire and Tumor Neoantigens

The immune repertoire exhibits high diversity and is crucial for studying adaptive immunity. Research also focuses on tumor neoantigens.

Mechanism of tumor neoantigen formation:

  • Originates from somatic mutations.
  • Mutant peptides are processed into 8-11 mer peptides.
  • Subsequently captured by MHC class I molecules.
  • Finally expressed on the surface of tumor cells, forming tumor neoantigens, thereby activating tumor-specific T cells.

1.3 TCR Research in the Context of COVID-19

In the context of the global COVID-19 pandemic, research on SARS-CoV-2-associated virus-specific TCRs and diverse immune repertoires is crucial. Key findings:

  • T cells can clear SARS virus in the absence of antibodies or when the innate immune system is not activated.
  • SARS-CoV-2-specific T cells persist, forming long-lasting memory responses.
  • T cell responses can activate humoral responses and may precede antibodies in asymptomatic or mild disease.

Core Research Content and Directions

This research includes bioinformatics analysis of TCR repertoires from COVID-19 patients and tumor neoantigen prediction.

Main research directions:

  • TCR repertoire characterization: Investigation of CDR3 clonotype features, diversity, V/J gene preferences, overlap, etc.
  • Classifier development and improvement: Construction of a COVID-19 symptom classifier, algorithm improvement for COVID-19-associated TCRs, interpretation of peptide contributions, and validation with additional datasets.
  • Study populations: Involves immune repertoire sequencing of peripheral blood T cells, covering acute phase, transition phase, recovery phase, healthy individuals, and vaccinated populations.

Chapter 2: Materials and Methods

2.1 Data Sources and Description

  • COVID-19 data: Derived from the ImmuneCODE database, including TCR sequences from over 1,400 subjects and more than 135,000 high-confidence SARS-CoV-2-specific TCRs.
  • Liver cancer data: Single-cell and exome sequencing data were obtained from the GSA-Human system with GSA accession numbers HRA001748 and HRA001749. The project included 124 liver cancer patients (79 with HCC) and 8 liver cancer mice.

2.2 Bioinformatics Analysis Pipeline

Immune repertoire analysis pipeline: Raw input → Alignment → Clone integration → VDJmatch → GLIPH → R language → Machine learning classifier.

Bioinformatics workflow for immune repertoire analysis
Technical description: The immune repertoire bioinformatics analysis pipeline is as follows: Patient sequences are aligned to the TCR reference genome in the International ImMunoGeneTics information system (IMGT) database. After identifying V, D, and J gene segment sequences, they are assembled into CDR3 amino acid sequences. VDJmatch can then be used to annotate TCRs with known antigen-specific epitopes. GLIPH can cluster TCRs with similar antigen specificities. R language is used for comprehensive analysis of the immune repertoire, and finally, machine learning classifiers are used for prediction.
Classifier construction method
Classifier construction method: For each TCR pair, we calculated its frequency across different groups and used Fisher's exact test on a 2×2 contingency table to assess the association between TCRs and phenotypic status, resulting in the COVID-19-associated TCR summary table shown. A P-value threshold was manually set, and sequences below the threshold were considered virus-related and classified as 1, while others were classified as 0 for model training.
Tumor neoantigen prediction pipeline flowchart

Tumor neoantigen prediction pipeline

  • Input data: Whole-exome sequencing (WES) data from tumor and normal tissues.
  • Somatic mutation detection: Alignment to the reference genome using tools such as Mutect2 to identify somatic mutations (e.g., SNVs, Indels).
  • HLA typing: Inference of patient HLA (Human Leukocyte Antigen) alleles using tools such as OptiType or HLAscan.
  • Candidate neoantigen prediction: Generation of mutant peptides from mutation information and prediction of their binding affinity (IC50 values) to the inferred HLA molecules using tools such as NetMHCpan.
  • Neoantigen filtering and selection: Selection of tumor neoantigens with high immunogenicity based on binding affinity thresholds (e.g., IC50 < 500 nM) and expression levels.

In contrast, the immune repertoires of acute phase and recovery phase patients differ significantly from healthy individuals, resulting in better classifier performance that can distinguish approximately 90% of the population.

Chapter 4: Summary and Discussion

This research focused on two main directions: COVID-19-associated immune repertoire analysis and tumor neoantigen prediction, successfully achieving the following objectives:

  • Revealing TCR repertoire characteristics: Conducted in-depth bioinformatics analysis of TCR repertoires from COVID-19 patients at different disease stages, revealing differences in TCRβ CDR3 clonotypes, diversity, and V/J gene preferences across different populations.
  • Constructing efficient classifiers: Successfully developed a machine learning classifier based on TCR sequences (using SVM-rbf algorithm) that can accurately distinguish COVID-19 patients from healthy populations, achieving excellent performance metrics in the validation set (AUC as high as 0.931313).
  • Predicting tumor neoantigens: Analyzed WES data from liver cancer patients, predicting potential tumor neoantigens and providing potential targets and research directions for personalized immunotherapy.

Future Work

Future research can be conducted in the following directions:

  • Classifier optimization and clinical translation: Integrate the TCR classifier into user-friendly clinical diagnostic tools, improve model generalization and robustness to achieve early and rapid assessment of SARS-CoV-2 infection status.
  • Neoantigen functional validation: Perform wet lab validation of predicted liver cancer neoantigens (e.g., in vitro T cell stimulation experiments) to confirm their immunogenicity and role in T cell activation.
  • Multi-omics data integration: Further integrate single-cell transcriptome, spatial transcriptome, and TCR repertoire data to deeply analyze the complex regulatory network between the tumor microenvironment and immune cell clonal expansion.
